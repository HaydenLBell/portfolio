About this project

Try enough versions of a trading strategy on the same historical data and one of them will look brilliant. That doesn't mean it works. It might just be the version that happened to line up with the noise, and a backtest on its own can't tell you which.

To see how bad this gets, I generated 1,000 strategies made of pure random noise, so every one of them had a true Sharpe ratio of zero, and backtested each over a year of daily data. The best one came out with an annualised Sharpe ratio of about 3.4. Shown on its own, that looks like a great find. It's just the luckiest of 1,000.

This tool takes the returns of every variant you tried, not just the winner, and estimates how much of the winner's performance is likely to be luck.

What it checks

  • Deflated Sharpe ratio: Starts from the probabilistic Sharpe ratio, which asks how confident you can be that a strategy's true Sharpe ratio is above a benchmark, given how much data you have and how skewed or fat-tailed the returns are. The deflated version raises that benchmark to the Sharpe ratio the best of N zero-skill strategies would reach by luck, so the more variants you try, the higher the bar.
  • Probability of backtest overfitting: Splits the history into equal blocks and tries every way of using half of them to pick a winner and the other half to check it (70 splits with 8 blocks). Each time, it sees where the in-sample winner ranks out of sample. PBO is how often it ends up in the bottom half.
  • A verdict: One report for the whole set of strategies tried: likely genuine, inconclusive, or likely overfit, with the numbers behind it.

The maths, briefly

The luck benchmark for N strategies comes from extreme value theory. It's roughly where the maximum of N draws from a bell curve is expected to land, scaled by how spread out the Sharpe ratios of all the trials were:

SR* = σ(SR) × [ (1 − γ) Φ⁻¹(1 − 1/N) + γ Φ⁻¹(1 − 1/(Ne)) ]

where γ ≈ 0.5772 is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. The deflated Sharpe ratio is then the probabilistic Sharpe ratio measured against SR* instead of zero.

For PBO, the in-sample winner's out-of-sample rank is turned into a relative rank and then a logit:

ω = rank / (N + 1), λ = ln(ω / (1 − ω))

A negative λ means the winner did worse than the median strategy on data it wasn't picked on. PBO is the share of splits where λ ≤ 0.

Testing it

Before trusting it on real strategies, I tested it on synthetic data where I already knew the right answer.

  • Pure noise: 1,000 noise strategies over a year gave a deflated Sharpe ratio of 0.54. A plain probabilistic Sharpe ratio against zero would call the same winner almost certainly real, because it doesn't know about the other 999. PBO on noise averaged around 0.5, as it should.
  • A planted edge: I added 0.2% a day to one strategy. With one year of data the detector picked it out but only gave a deflated Sharpe ratio of 0.75, not enough to call it. With ten years it reached 1.00.
  • A realistic edge: Shrinking the edge to 0.02% a day (an annual Sharpe ratio of about 0.3) meant even ten years of data couldn't confirm it. With 1,000 variants in play, a small real edge just gets lost in the luck.

That last result was the most useful thing I've learned so far. The more ideas you test, the bigger an edge has to be before you can tell it apart from chance.

What I'm working on

Next is running it on real research: a sweep of moving-average strategies on 15 years of S&P 500 data, to see whether the best combination survives. After that, unit tests, and rewriting the cross-validation loop in C++ with Python bindings. With 16 blocks it has 12,870 splits to get through, so it's the obvious bottleneck.

Small samples are still the hard case. With a few years of daily data the statistics get noisy, and even PBO itself varies a lot from one dataset to the next. The tool needs to say when it can't tell, rather than give a confident answer it can't back up.
